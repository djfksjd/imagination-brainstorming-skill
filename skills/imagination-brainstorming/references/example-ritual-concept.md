# The early goodbye

A closing rite for a bakery that has traded on one street for ninety years. Written with the
`imagination-brainstorming` skill; this file and `example-ritual-concept.json` are the pair
`spec_gate.py` reads. Shipped as the worked example that is not a product or a service.

<!-- section: brief -->
## What was asked for

The ask was a ceremony for the last day of a bakery that has traded on the same street for ninety years. What the session established is that the family are not looking for a way to mark the ending; they are looking for a way to survive it, and the last day is the part they are most afraid of.

The difference between those two sentences is the whole session. A ceremony that marks an
ending and a ceremony that makes an ending survivable are not the same object, and the first
three questions were spent finding out which one had been asked for.

## Premises

| Premise | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The ritual happens on the last trading day. | inverted | Asked what the last day would feel like, the daughter said she had been dreading it for a year. The date is the thing carrying the fear, not the closing. Moving the farewell earlier is the only change that touches that, and it costs nothing anyone was attached to. |
| The ritual is for the customers. | deleted | The customers will be fine; they will buy bread somewhere else by Friday. The people who have to live with this are the three family members and the two staff, and designing for the crowd was quietly designing for the least affected party in the street. |
| Something has to be kept. | kept | Tested and it held. Asked what she would want in twenty years, the mother named the order book rather than the sign, because it has her father's handwriting in it. Keeping is real here; what was wrong was assuming the object had to be handed over in front of an audience. |
| The shop's ending is one event. | inverted | There are three endings and they were being collapsed into one: the last sale, the last day the family are in the building, and the day the lease changes hands. Separating them gave each one a size, and only the first turned out to want a ceremony at all. |

Two premises were inverted and one deleted. The one that survived - that something has to be
kept - survived a real test rather than going unexamined: it was the object's audience that was
wrong, not the keeping.

## What is off the table

The twelve answers that came cheapest to this brief, agreed with the family before anything was
designed:

- a last day open house with free bread
- a farewell party on the final evening
- a memory wall of photographs
- a guest book for regulars to sign
- the sign is taken down and handed to the founder's grandchild
- a commemorative plaque on the shopfront
- a final bake of the oldest recipe
- a local newspaper piece about ninety years
- a video interview with the family
- the recipe book donated to the town archive
- a candle-lit vigil outside the shop at closing time
- a limited edition tote bag

**The skeleton they share:** A gathering held on the final trading day at which the shop is thanked, photographed and remembered aloud, ending with an object handed over to be kept.

The family added their own two: nothing that turns our closing into a public event for strangers to attend; and no crying in front of the counter staff on their last shift.
Both are too long to match literally, so each is answered in writing in the sidecar's
`banlist_contract.manual_checks_cleared`. Eight of the twelve instincts above are also too long
to match, and those are a reread rather than a written answer - the gate names them and does not
fail on them, because only the user's own exclusions are answered in writing.

## Approaches considered

Run 1 dealt `designed-for-repair` into the unsafe seat. A rite that happens once cannot break
often, has no maker to be absent, and has no spare parts; the frame could not be occupied by this
brief at all, so the hand was redealt at `--run 2` rather than arguing a frame the brief cannot
hold. That is what `frame_fit` is for and it is the first thing it caught here.

### the-early-goodbye **(chosen)**

*Frame: `reverse-order`.* The farewell is held six weeks before the shutters come down for good, and the last trading day is deliberately left as an ordinary Tuesday: same opening hour, same bread, no speeches. Everyone who wants to say something says it while the shop is still working, so the goodbye happens inside the life of the place rather than over its body. The family spends the six weeks afterwards winding the business up in peace, and the street watches the shop simply stop rather than end.

**What occupies the frame:** The inverted order is safe enough to accept because the shop is still trading when the goodbye happens: anyone who finds the farewell premature can come back the following Tuesday and buy a loaf, which is the proof that nothing has been taken away early.

**How it fails here:** The last day arrives anyway. At three in the afternoon the mother is alone behind a counter nobody came to, six weeks after the town already said goodbye, and the ending she has to live through is the one nobody designed. The rite protected the family from a public last day and left them with a private one they were told would not matter.

### the-inheritors-clause **(unsafe seat)**

*Frame: `the-absent-party`.* The rite is written for the people who cannot be in the room: whoever takes the lease next, the customers who moved away decades ago, and the grandchildren who never worked a shift here. It consists of three obligations the family accepts in front of witnesses - what is left in the shop for the next tenant, what is sent to those who left the street, and what the family may never sell. The people present are there to hold the family to promises made to people who are not.

**What occupies the frame:** The absent parties' interest is enforced by a written clause in the lease handover and a letter held by the family solicitor, both read out on the night, so the promise survives the evening that made it rather than depending on anyone's memory of a feeling.

**How it fails here:** Nobody in the room wants it. The family are asked to spend their last evening making binding promises to a tenant they have not met and grandchildren who may never care, and the neighbours who came to say thank you find themselves at a legal proceeding. The obligations are accepted, quietly resented, and abandoned within a year.

### the-refusal-list

*Frame: `abuse-case-first`.* The worst realistic use decides the shape: a ninety-year closing is exactly the kind of story a developer, a councillor or a local paper will borrow for its own purposes. So the rite is built as a set of refusals, agreed and signed before anything else is planned - who may photograph, who may speak, what may be quoted, and what happens to the shop's name afterwards. Whatever survives the refusals is the ceremony.

**What occupies the frame:** The harm is the shop's ending being used as material by someone with a use for it, and the structural feature that blocks it is that the right to speak, photograph and quote is granted in writing beforehand by the family, and by nobody else, for one evening only.

**How it fails here:** A farewell defined by what is forbidden is a joyless one. The family spend the evening policing a list instead of being thanked, the neighbours feel accused of an intention they never had, and the warmth the closing was supposed to hold is spent on enforcement.

## The concept

Two acts, six weeks apart, with different sizes and different audiences. The first is a Thursday
evening in October with the shutters down for two hours and twenty-two invited people inside,
built around the order book rather than around a speech. The second is the last trading day
itself, which is an ordinary Tuesday nobody has been told about.

**How it survives its own failure mode:** The stated death of this approach is the unattended afternoon: the town says goodbye in October and the mother is alone behind the counter in December. So the design does not end at the early evening. The last trading day carries a second, private act with three rules - the shop opens at its ordinary hour, the family bake only what they would have baked anyway, and at closing the youngest of them writes the final line in the order book and reads it aloud to whoever is in the room, which is never more than five people. What would falsify this: if the family find themselves inviting neighbours to that second act, the weight has moved back onto the last day and the early evening was decoration.

**What it forbids:**

<!-- bind: chosen.forbids -->
It forbids the last trading day from being announced, advertised or photographed by anyone, including the family, and it forbids any speech, gift or presentation being made across the counter while the shop is trading. The cost is real: the regulars who only find out afterwards will feel they were not given the chance to say thank you, and some of them will say so.
<!-- /bind -->

**What is now impossible:**

<!-- bind: chosen.impossible_now -->
A stranger arriving on the last day to witness the ending becomes impossible, because nobody outside the invitation knows which day it is.
<!-- /bind -->

**The boring half:** the twenty-two names and who writes each invitation; the two hours the shop
is shut and what that costs on a Thursday; telling the staff; the six weeks of ordinary trading
afterwards, stocked and priced as any other six weeks; and the line in the order book, which
somebody has to have decided on before the Tuesday arrives.

<!-- section: first-use -->
## First contact

<!-- bind: first_use_scene -->
A Thursday evening in October, the shop shut for two hours, twenty-two people inside and the ovens still warm. The father has been talked out of a speech and instead stands at the end of the counter reading out four dates from the order book: the week of the flood, the morning the street was closed for the parade, the first day his daughter served alone, and the day the second oven was installed. Between the dates people call out what they remember; a woman near the door corrects him about the flood and he writes the correction in the margin in pencil. Nothing is handed to anyone. At half past eight the shutters go up again and the shop opens as usual on Friday at six, which is the point of the evening and the part nobody quite believes until it happens. Six weeks later, on a Tuesday nobody has been told about, the daughter serves the last customer, writes one line under the four dates, and reads it to her mother and the two staff.
<!-- /bind -->

## Nearest existing things

- **A retirement party held on someone's last working day.** The retirement party happens on the day it is about, which is the arrangement this design refuses; here the gathering is deliberately six weeks early and the actual last day is left unmarked and unannounced.
- **A church's final service before a building is deconsecrated.** The final service is the ending, performed in public and often with the press invited. This separates the two: the public act happens while the place is still in ordinary use, and the ending itself is private and unattended by design.
- **A 'closing down' sale.** A closing sale advertises the ending to increase takings and turns the last weeks into a performance of decline. Here the last six weeks are priced and stocked exactly as any other six weeks, and the ending is never used to sell anything.

None of these is a new invention and this one is not either; what is unusual here is only the
separation of the public act from the ending it is about.

## Handoff

**Next: stop.** The deliverable was the shape of the two acts and the refusals around them. What remains is the family's own work - the date, the list of twenty-two names, and the conversation with the staff - and none of it wants a further spec.

This spec does not cover the winding up of the business, the lease, or what happens to the shop's
name after the family leave the street. None of those is a design question.
